# HEMA Parrying / Blocking Research Findings

## 1. Terminology Distinctions (German Tradition)

The German Liechtenauer tradition makes **sharp distinctions** between concepts that English often conflates as "parry/block":

| German Term | Common Translation | Actual Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Versetzen** | Parry / Displacement | Active deflection — redirecting opponent's blade while maintaining your own line of attack. NOT a passive block. |
| **Absetzen** | Setting Aside | Luring out and hindering cuts/thrusts; proactive interception |
| **Nachreisen** | Pursuit / Following After | Pressing into the bind after initial contact to maintain pressure |
| **Überlaufen** | Overrunning | Flowing over opponent's attack when they aim low, shaming them |
| **Schneiden** | Slicing off | Cutting away hard attacks from below (one of 4 slices: 2 high, 2 low) |
| **Hendetrucken / Drücken** | Pressing Hands | Turning edge to flat and crushing opponent's hands in the bind |
| **Binden** | Binding | The state when swords touch — neither parry nor block but a mutual engagement |
| **Winden** | Winding | Rotating sword in the bind to change edge orientation while staying engaged |
| **Durchwechseln / Durchlaufen** | Changing Through / Running Through | Thrusting through the bind when opponent binds; leads directly to wrestling |

**Critical insight:** Liechtenauer explicitly says (couplet 71): *"Beware of parrying [Versetzen]. If it happens to you, it troubles you greatly."* The tradition considers passive parrying a **mistake** — you should be proactive, not reactive. This is fundamentally different from modern sport fencing where a simple parry-riposte is standard.

## 2. Core Philosophy: Provoker vs. Receiver

Liechtenauer's system is built on **never being the one who reacts to an attack**. The key verses:

> *"He who follows the strokes, should rejoice little in his art."* (v.11)
> *"Strike close whatever you will, no changer will come into your shield."* (v.12)
> *"If you would bring the art to light, Go forth with left and cut with right."* (v.9)

**The model:** You attack first (provoker), opponent must parry/defend (receiver). The provoker then has all the options — winding, pressing, changing through, wrestling. This is not a "parry percentage" system but a **initiative system**.

## 3. Blade Contact Physics & Mechanics

### Blade Anatomy (from Meyer §3 / Tobler translation)
- **Strong (Stercke):** Grip to middle of blade — the powerful portion used for binding and pressing
- **Weak (Schweche):** Middle to tip — used for attacks, thrusts, and threatening openings
- The bind occurs when swords contact in the strong-to-strong or strong-to-middle zone

### Hard vs. Soft Bind (v.30, v.77, v.109)
> *"Cut, thrust, then hard or soft it lies"* — at every bind you must **feel** whether opponent is pressing hard (resisting) or softly (yielding), and respond accordingly.

### The Five Words of Liechtenauer (v.17-18 + ms. 3227a notes)
The foundational concepts for blade contact:
1. **Vor** (Before) — attacking first, initiating
2. **Nach** (After) — responding to opponent's attack  
3. **Stärke** (Strong) — your strong against their weak
4. **Schwäche** (Weak) — accepting weak-to-weak to access other options
5. **Indes** (Within/Meanwhile) — the instant of blade contact where decisions are made

> *"The word 'within' cuts sharp as steel."* (v.78, Chidester translation)

**"Indes"** is the temporal/spatial concept at the moment blades meet — not a specific technique but the decision point for winding, pressing, pulling, or changing through.

## 4. The "Fool's Parry" and Counter-Parrying Strategy

> *"A fool will parry all strikes, so Pursue, run down, and set the blow."* (v.24)

Liechtenauer explicitly mocks **passive parrying**. When opponent attempts to deflect your strike:
- **Nachreisen** (Pursuit/Following After): Press into their blade with continued pressure
- **Überlaufen** (Overrunning): If they aim low, flow over them from above
- **Absetzen** (Setting Aside): Lure and intercept — "Learn to lure them out at will, then hinder cuts and thrusts with skill" (v.83)

### The Four Oppositions / Displacements (v.70-74)
> *"Four are the oppositions [Versetzen], which hurt the four guards very much."*
> *"Should you become displaced at last, then listen now to what I say: Wrench off, cut in, and don't delay."*

When you ARE parried (which is framed as a failure), the response is **not** a simple riposte but:
1. **Wrench off** (Ablaufen) — pull blade away from bad position
2. **Cut in quickly with surprise** (v.73)
3. **Set on to four extremities** — re-establish guard positions

## 5. Binding, Pressing & Waffenfühlen (Blade Feel)

### Hand Crushing / Pressing (v.95)
> *"Turn your edge to the flat and press the hands."*

This is a specific technique: when blades are bound, you rotate from cutting edge to flat surface and **crush opponent's hands** against theirs. This is both a strike and a control mechanism.

### Pulling / Zucken (v.88-90)
> *"Step close in binding. The pulling provides good finds."*
> *"Pull — if he meets, pull more. Work to find: that will hurt him."*

Retreating blade while maintaining contact — a probing action to discover openings. Repeated pulling when met builds pressure and creates opportunities.

### Winding / Winden (v.26, v.104-108)
> *"Hang, wind with the openings"* (v.26)
> *"Who properly well hangs and executes the windings as well, and the eight windings has also correctly considered."* (v.105-107)

Rotating your sword in the bind to change edge orientation while staying engaged — this is where **Waffenfühlen** (blade feel/awareness) is critical. Eight primary winds × 3 variations each = 24 total winding options from either side.

### The "Speaking Window" / Sprechfenster (v.98-101)
> *"Execute the speaking window; stand blithely and look at his actions."*

An opening created in your guard that invites opponent attack, then snaps shut to strike — a tactical feint through blade positioning.

## 6. Transition to Wrestling from Sword Play

The German tradition has **multiple explicit pathways** from sword engagement to grappling:

### Pathway 1: Running Through → Direct Grapple
> *"Run through, let it hang with the pommel. Grasp if you want to wrestle."* (v.91)
> *"When strength would press and oppress you, remember this: just run on through."* (v.92)

When opponent presses hard in bind, drive forward past their blade — your sword hangs at pommel height and you immediately grapple. This is the **most common** transition described.

### Pathway 2: The Thwart/Reverser → Wrestling (v.54)
> *"The reverser enforces the running through and also brings wrestling."*

Executing a thwart cut (Zwerchhaw) that deflects downward, then forcing through to grapple position.

### Pathway 3: Taking the Elbow (v.55)
> *"Take the elbow certainly, spring into his balance."*

After displacing opponent's blade, grab their elbow and disrupt stance — immediate transition to wrestling control.

### Pathway 4: Ott Jud's Arm Control Grip
Ott Jud's treatise provides a **specific grip for initiating wrestling from standing**:

> *"When you wish to wrestle your opponent by holding his arms, always remember to hold him at the muscle of his right arm with your left hand, and with your right hand on the outside of his left... push him backwards forcefully, and with your right hand grab hold in front of his left hand pull it towards you."*

This **arm-cross grip** (left hand on opponent's bicep/right arm, right hand on opponent's outer left arm/forearm) creates a position where all subsequent wrestling techniques can be applied. This is the foundational "catch" for transitioning from armed to unarmed combat.

## 7. Ott Jud's Three Principles of Wrestling (v.2)

> *"In all wrestling there are three things one must pay great attention to: the first is skill, the second speed, and the third is how to use your strength at the right place and time."*
> *"By far speed is the best, since speed allows for no countermoves."*

### Strategic Application:
- **Against weaker opponent:** Attack first (use speed)
- **Against equal opponent:** Simultaneous engagement (use balance/scales)
- **Against stronger opponent:** Wait for their attack, then target knees/balance points

> *"When you attack first use your speed, when you attack at the same time as the opponent use your balance and when you act after your opponent pay attention to his knee-bendings."*

This is a complete **combat decision tree** — not just "parry or block" but an entire strategy based on relative strength assessment.

## 8. Meyer's Expanded Handworks (1570, §11 List)

Meyer catalogs **34 distinct hand techniques** for blade contact situations:

Binding On (Annbinden), Remaining/Staying (Bleiben), Slicing/Schneiden, Striking Around (Umbschlagen), Pursuit/Following After (Nachreisen), Snapping Around (Umbschnappen), Running Off (Ablaufen), Doubling (Dopplern), Deceiving (Verfieren), Flitting (Verfliegen), Failing/Missing (Verfelen), Circling (Zierkel), Looping/Rinding (Rinde), Winding (Winden), Winding Through (Durchwinden), Reversing (Verkeren), Changing Through (Durchwechseln), Running Over (Überlaufen), Setting Aside (Absetzen), Cutting Away (Abschneiden), Pulling (Zucken), Hand Pushing (Hendetrucken), Sliding (Verschieben), Hanging (Verhengen), Barring/Blocking (Verstiellen), Barring/Shielding (Speren), Wrenching Out (Ausreißen), Gripping Over (Übergreifen), Weak Pushing (Schwachstoßen)

Plus **6 forbidden cuts** and **5 master cuts**, showing the depth of the system.

## 9. Key Differences from Modern Sport Fencing

| Aspect | HEMA German Tradition | Modern Sport Fencing |
|---|---|---|
| Primary defense | Proactive interception (Absetzen) | Reactive parry (Versetzen) |
| Blade contact | Embraced (Binden is central) | Avoided or minimal |
| Parrying | Actively discouraged as passive | Core defensive technique |
| Post-parry action | Wind/press/change/wrestle | Simple riposte line |
| Strength model | Strong vs. weak blade zones | Linear parry angles |
| Wrestling | Expected outcome of bind | Separate discipline entirely |

**No quantitative "parry success rate" exists in the historical sources.** The tradition operates on a qualitative model: if you're doing it correctly (as provoker/initiator), you shouldn't need to parry at all. If you ARE parried, it's considered an error that requires immediate correction through wrenching off and re-establishing initiative.
